How it works

From camera to fingertips in four steps

Our device bridges the gap between printed text and tactile reading through a streamlined pipeline.

Step 01
📷

Capture

The user presses a button to photograph any printed text — a book page, a sign, a restaurant menu.

Step 02
🔍

Recognize

OCR software identifies every character in the image, building a string of recognized text with high accuracy.

Step 03
⚙️

Translate

Each character is mapped to its corresponding Braille pattern — a 6-dot configuration unique to every letter and symbol.

Step 04
✋

Output

Solenoid pins raise and lower to form tactile Braille cells, 10 characters at a time, for the user to read by touch.

Two approaches, one mission

We're evaluating two core platform options. Both share the same solenoid output stage, speaker, and button interface — they differ in processing power and camera capability.

Option A

Arduino Uno R4 + Arducam

Lightweight, lower cost, proven ecosystem

  • 🔧

    Arduino Uno R4 WiFi

    Renesas RA4M1 @ 48MHz, 256KB flash. Handles solenoid control and serial communication.

  • 📸

    Arducam OV2640

    2MP camera module with SPI interface. Adequate resolution for text capture at close range.

48MHz CPU 256KB Flash 2MP Camera SPI Interface 5V Logic

Raspberry Pi 5 + ZeroCam

Full Linux OS, onboard OCR, superior image processing

  • 🖥️

    5MP camera with CSI ribbon connector. Fast, low-latency image capture with excellent text clarity.

The solenoid Braille cell

Each Braille cell consists of 6 solenoid-driven pins arranged in a 2×3 grid. When energized, a pin raises above the surface to create a tactile dot. The combination of raised and lowered pins encodes a specific character.

Our final device uses 10 cells in a row, allowing the user to read 10 characters at a time. A button press advances to the next set of characters, scrolling through the recognized text.

Pipeline breakdown

1

Image Acquisition

When the user presses the tactile button, the camera captures a high-resolution image. On the Pi 5, we use libcamera for fast, low-latency capture via the CSI bus. On the Arduino path, the Arducam buffers a JPEG over SPI.

libcamera CSI / SPI auto-focus
2

Preprocessing

The raw image is converted to grayscale, thresholded for contrast, and de-skewed if the camera angle is off. This dramatically improves OCR accuracy on imperfect captures.

OpenCV grayscale adaptive threshold deskew
3

Optical Character Recognition

The preprocessed image is fed into Tesseract OCR (or EasyOCR for more complex scripts). The engine returns a string of recognized characters with confidence scores. We filter low-confidence results.

Tesseract 5 EasyOCR Python confidence filtering
4

Braille Translation

Each character is mapped to its Grade 1 Braille equivalent — a 6-bit pattern representing which of the 6 pins should be raised. The lookup table covers A–Z, 0–9, and common punctuation.

Grade 1 Braille 6-bit encoding lookup table
5

Solenoid Output

The 6-bit patterns are serialized and sent to the solenoid driver board. Shift registers fan out the signals to control all 60 pins. The first 10 characters are displayed, and the user advances with the button.

shift registers GPIO serial 60 channels
6

Audio Feedback

The speaker provides confirmation beeps for button presses, announces when text has been captured, and can optionally read text aloud via TTS for users who prefer auditory feedback alongside tactile.

pyttsx3 espeak WAV playback
About

About the project

Built by students at Vernon Hills High School for Samsung Solve for Tomorrow 2025.

Our mission

BrailleVision was born from a simple question: why is it so hard for visually impaired people to read everyday printed text? Commercial Braille displays exist, but they cost thousands of dollars and are out of reach for most families and schools.

We're a team of high school students at Vernon Hills High School competing in Samsung's Solve for Tomorrow competition. Our goal is to prototype a device that can photograph any piece of printed text, run it through optical character recognition, and output the result as raised Braille pins using affordable solenoids.

The device is designed to be portable, battery-powered, and simple to operate with a single button. The final version features a row of 10 Braille cells (60 solenoid pins total), letting users read text 10 characters at a time by scrolling through with a button press.

We believe accessible technology should be affordable. Our target cost for the full device is under $100 — a fraction of commercial alternatives that can cost $10,000 or more.

Project timeline

Phase 1

Research & Planning

Identified the problem, researched existing solutions, defined hardware options and project scope.

Phase 2

Proof of Concept

Single Braille cell prototype with 6 solenoids. Validated OCR pipeline on Raspberry Pi with test images.

Phase 3

Full Prototype

Scaling to 10-cell row. Designing custom solenoid driver board, enclosure, and integrated button/speaker.

Phase 4

Testing & Submission

User testing with visually impaired individuals, refinement, documentation, and Samsung SFT submission.
